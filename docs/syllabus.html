Course Description

Generative AI moved into ordinary business work faster than any recent technology. McKinsey’s global survey found the share of organizations regularly using generative AI in at least one business function rose from roughly a third in 2023 to about two-thirds in early 2024, and to 71 percent by 2025. The Census Bureau’s Business Trends and Outlook Survey, which asks the narrower question of whether a firm used AI to produce a good or service in the past two weeks, put the national rate at 19.8 percent in May 2026, up from roughly 5 percent in late 2023 and about twice as high in information (39.7 percent) and in finance and insurance (33.9 percent). The gap between those two numbers is itself informative: many people are using these tools, and far fewer organizations have built anything durable around them.

Business uses fall into a few broad categories. There is individual productivity — the drafting, summarizing, analysis, and coding that one person does faster with a model beside them. There is internal deployment — putting a model to work on the firm’s own documents and systems, in workflows that several people depend on. There are customer-facing applications, where the model talks to people outside the firm. And there is the reshaping of the product itself, where AI is what is being sold rather than what makes it. The categories differ less in the technology than in what goes wrong: a bad answer you catch yourself is a nuisance, a bad answer inside a workflow is a process failure, and a bad answer in front of a customer is a liability.

This course covers the first two. We start with individual productivity, because the judgment you need there — knowing what the model is doing, what it is good at, how it fails, and how to check it — is the same judgment every larger deployment rests on. We then move to work that outlives the session you did it in: writing a repeated task down so others can run it, connecting a model to data it was never trained on, generating the document or application someone else opens, and assembling an agent with a deliberately chosen list of things it can do. The course closes on the questions a firm has to answer before switching any of this on — where the data goes, what an outside document can talk the system into doing, and what it costs to run.

The course is hands-on throughout. You work in your own container on the course lab server, and you show what you built to the room.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

  1. Work fluently with generative AI in your own tasks — choosing the right tool for the job, directing it well, and verifying what it gives back, with a working understanding of how these models produce their answers and where that mechanism fails.
  2. Turn ad hoc use into something durable — a documented procedure, a connection to your own data, a deliverable someone else opens, or an agent with a defined job — that a colleague can run without you.
  3. Evaluate a proposed deployment on the terms a firm actually decides on: which model and where it runs, what data it touches, what it is permitted to do, and what it costs relative to what it saves.
